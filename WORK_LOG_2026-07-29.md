## 2. What I did, in order, and why

### 2.1 Set the quality bar before accepting any output

The first thing I did was refuse to accept a polished-looking draft as finished. I asked for a structured adversarial review: multiple independent critics attacking the document from different angles, then a **separate** verifier applying a strict pass/fail gate, looping until it passed.

**Why I did this:** a document that looks professional is not the same as a document that is correct. I had no way to personally audit 20 requirements for internal consistency, so I built a process that would do it instead of trusting my own read-through. This was a deliberate choice to spend more time up front rather than discover defects in front of Vincent.

**What it caught:** broken internal cross-references (section pointers aimed at sections that did not exist), requirements labelled as verified fact that were actually unproven assumptions, and one requirement with no test behind it at all. If I had shipped the version I started the day with, all of that would have gone to review with my name on it.

### 2.2 Cut it to four pages and stripped the ticket framing

I decided the deliverable had to be **four pages**, readable in one sitting, with **no ticket or user-story references** anywhere in the content.

**Why:** the audience is a CPSO and a technical lead. Neither will read a fifteen-page document carefully, and both think in terms of the product, not the backlog. A document full of story IDs reads as process paperwork; a document that just explains the feature reads as product thinking. I also wanted it to survive being forwarded to someone who has never seen the ticket.

**The tradeoff I accepted:** four pages cannot hold acceptance criteria and a test matrix. I knowingly gave those up for readability, and dealt with the consequence separately (see 2.3).

### 2.3 Judged the deliverable as incomplete and fixed the gap

When I asked whether the 4-page version was a complete deliverable, the honest answer was no — engineering could build from it, but **QA could not test from it**, because the traceability and test matrix had been cut for length.

**My decision:** hand in both documents — the brief for everyone, and a detailed engineering/QA companion behind it. I decided the "one clean file" preference was worth breaking here because the two documents serve genuinely different readers.

**The condition I attached:** the long version could not simply be restored, because it still contained the defects found in 2.1 and would have **contradicted** the corrected brief. I required it to be rebuilt to the same standard first, so the two documents reinforce each other instead of undermining each other. Two documents that disagree are worse than one document.

### 2.4 Took a correction from Vincent and forced it through the whole spec

I had asked Vincent directly whether we could flag a fictional incident as training data and keep it out of operational feeds. His answer was the single most important input of the day:

> "Put it in archive mode (soft delete). We need to update the reporting layer to exclude this data… **But again be aware these are still visible in prod. Filtering is an enhancement that needs to be carried out at a later stage.**"

The draft at that point claimed practice data was "excluded from every operational consumer throughout its lifecycle" — stated as settled fact. **That was false.** The flag exists; the filter that actually hides flagged data does not.

**What I did:** stopped, and had the correction propagated through every place the topic appeared in both documents — the summary, the requirement rows, the isolation section, rollout, definition of done, and the evidence basis. The spec now says plainly: the flag exists, the filter is not built, archived data is still visible in production, **production go-live is blocked** until the filter is built and verified, and until then this runs only in DEV/UAT.

**Why this mattered so much:** if I had handed Vincent a document claiming isolation already worked, I would have been telling the person who corrected me that I had not listened — and worse, someone could have shipped a feature that puts fake "stolen laptop" incidents into live guard feeds. This turned a hidden risk into the most visible statement in the document. It is now the thing a reviewer sees first.

### 2.5 Challenged my own claim before it went in an email

When drafting the covering email, I stopped and asked whether it was actually true that we had figured out SAM's behaviour from the DEV management console.

**Why I asked:** I was about to put a factual claim about my own investigation in writing to two senior colleagues. If Jeroen had asked me a follow-up question about it, I needed to be able to answer.

**What the check found:** it was a mix, not one source. The console and UAT reporting page proved that activities can be edited and archived, and that no onboarding-completion field exists today. SAM's actual behaviour — including that corrections can be written through conversation context — came from the source documentation and the template pack. Claiming the console alone explained SAM would have been an overclaim.

I rewrote the email to credit both sources accurately. Small thing, but it is exactly the kind of loose claim that gets picked apart in a review meeting.

### 2.6 Rejected the visual design

The first design used dark near-black table headers, zebra striping and filled colour badges. I rejected it: it was visually uneasy, hard to read, and looked machine-generated rather than like something a person had written.

**Why this is a real judgement and not fussiness:** the document's job is to be read by two busy senior people. A dense, heavy layout gets skimmed. It also signals "generated output," which undermines the credibility of the content regardless of how good the content is.

I asked for calm and editorial instead — and the redesign (light headers with a single accent underline, no zebra, quiet text labels instead of filled badges, one restrained accent colour, serif headings, more whitespace) is the version going out.

### 2.7 Took Jeroen's review and made both changes real

Jeroen raised two points:

1. **Engagement.** The guard has just received a tool that makes their life easier, so it should feel like receiving a gift — "Congratulations… I'm your new assistant…" — and certainly not a dry "now do this, then that."
2. **The urgent-incident flow was over-weighted.** In practice a guard handles a real emergency the way they always have, and new guards are accompanied early on. Not a problem to have the flow; just not important.

**How I handled the engagement point:** I did not want it to become soft language that gets dropped in build. I required it to be a **real requirement with a fail condition** — the spec now states outright that a dry "do this, then that" sequence does not meet the requirement, and it has an acceptance criterion and a test with a pass threshold (product sign-off plus four of five test guards calling it welcoming rather than a test).

**One judgement call I made against the literal feedback:** Jeroen's "Congratulations…" was a sketch of the feeling he wanted. An early pass transcribed it literally, and I agreed with the argument that congratulating an adult for being issued mandatory software reads slightly off. So the *requirement* now asks for a warm personal greeting that says what SAM does for the guard and why it helps, and leaves the exact line to copy polish. I flagged this openly rather than silently changing his wording — if he wants it verbatim, that is his call to make.

**How I handled the urgent-incident point:** cut it to one minor, low-priority requirement per document, with his own reasoning written into the spec so nobody quietly re-inflates it later. I deliberately did **not** delete it, because he said the flow itself was not a problem. Following feedback correctly includes not over-correcting.

### 2.8 Walked the flow as a real guard and found it incomplete

This was the piece of work I am most satisfied with. I read the step-by-step flow line by line, imagining an actual guard on an actual first shift, and concluded it did not hold up.

Five things were wrong or missing:

- The guard was never told the ground rules — that it is required, that the incident is pretend, that progress saves. This had been lost when the intro was rewritten to be warmer.
- Nothing checked that the microphone actually **worked**, only that permission was granted. A faulty mic or a noisy site would be discovered at the worst moment.
- The guard was never shown **how** to talk to SAM — the Talk gesture was never taught.
- The guard was asked to correct a report **they had never seen**. "Change Dell to MacBook" only makes sense if the report is in front of them — and seeing it is also the moment the tool proves its value.
- The ending dropped them nowhere.

All five are now fixed. This was not something the AI raised; it came from thinking about the actual human doing the actual task.

### 2.9 Specified the real setup sequence

I described what actually has to happen at setup, which the spec had oversimplified into "enable the microphone": a link that opens SAM OnSite's permissions in the phone's settings, the guard enables everything and returns, says or types "done" — and then **SAM verifies rather than believing them** — followed by the same pattern for NFC.

**Two things I insisted on:**

- **SAM checks, it does not trust.** If something is still off, it names which one. This is consistent with how the rest of the spec treats completion (observed, never self-reported), so it fits the document's logic rather than being a special case.
- **The "done" must be typeable.** Before microphone permission is granted, the guard physically cannot speak — so if the build only listens for voice at that moment, the guard is stuck at the very first gate with no way forward. This is exactly the kind of gap that ships as a bug, and it now has its own test.

### 2.10 Supplied the real interface and let it correct the spec

I provided actual screenshots of the app and settings screens. These changed the specification in ways no amount of drafting would have caught:

| What the screenshot showed | What it changed in the spec |
|---|---|
| Permissions have **levels** ("only while using", "ask every time", location "all the time") | Verification must check the *level*, not just on/off |
| Android's "Manage app if unused" auto-removes camera/location/mic after months | Setup must switch this off, or access is silently lost later |
| A *question* ("How do I hand out a key?") was recorded as a Procedure activity | "Asking creates no report" was **false** — the spec now says nothing *live* may result; any record is suppressed or training-flagged |
| Real transcripts show supervisor alerts, KPI impact, and Pronect Action Tracker items | Those are named live consumers the exclusion filter must cover |
| The Location screen already has "Permissions needed" and "Open system settings" | Permission-state reading and the settings link partly exist — softens an open engineering question |

The third row is the one that matters most: it meant a claim in my own document was wrong, and I found it by supplying evidence rather than by arguing. The verifier then caught two more places where that old absolute claim had survived, which is why the review loop ran twice.

I also noticed, from the first (mockup) set of screens, that the Home/Activity/Settings navigation bar was visible during onboarding — which would be a skip route straight past a mandatory gate. Flagged for whoever builds the screens.

### 2.11 Learned the material rather than outsourcing understanding

I deliberately spent time being taught the content, because I will be in a room with Vincent and Jeroen and "the AI wrote it" is not an answer.

I pushed for the right level three times — first the concepts, then the technical layer, then a rebuild from zero when the technical version assumed too much. That was me managing my own learning, not passively receiving explanation.

**Where I got to:** I can now explain, in my own words and without notes, why practice data is a risk, what a tag and a filter are and why the missing filter blocks production, why the flag must be applied at creation rather than cleaned up afterwards (an alert or webhook cannot be un-sent), why completion is decided server-side rather than trusted from the phone, and what fail-closed means and why refusing beats leaking.

**Vocabulary I now hold well enough to follow an engineering conversation:** backend, database, table/row/column, query, filter, view / row-level rule, transaction, webhook, server-side vs client-side, idempotency, concurrency, fail-closed, DEV/UAT/production, feature flag.

**Honest boundary:** I understand these at the level of a product owner who can follow the discussion, ask the right question and know who owns the answer. I could not implement any of it, and I will not pretend otherwise in a review. What I *can* do is state the requirement, state the risk, and say "the exact route is for engineering to confirm" — which is the correct answer, not a weak one.

### 2.12 Built for continuity

I had a full handoff document produced and committed to the repository, capturing the ticket, every confirmed decision, Vincent's correction, the real-interface facts, the working method, the open engineering questions and what remains outstanding — so this work does not live only in my head or in a chat window.

## 3. Decisions I made today, and the reasoning

| Decision | Reasoning |
|---|---|
| Require adversarial review + a separate verifier before accepting anything | Polished ≠ correct; I could not personally audit 20 requirements for consistency |
| Four pages, no ticket references | Senior audience, must survive forwarding, must be read in one sitting |
| Ship two documents, not one | Engineering and QA need traceability the brief cannot hold |

| Keep unknowns labelled as unknowns | Inventing a field name or endpoint would be found out immediately and would poison the rest |

## 4. How I used AI, honestly

**What I delegated:**

- Adversarial review from multiple angles, and independent pass/fail verification

**What I kept:**

- The quality bar, and refusing output until it met it
- Stakeholder relationships and interpreting what Vincent and Jeroen actually meant
- Evidence judgement — what we can claim, what must stay labelled unproven

- The real interface, which corrected the spec
- Editorial and design judgement — rejecting the layout and the first email
- Deciding what goes to whom, and when

**Where my input changed the outcome materially:**
1. Vincent's correction — without it, the spec would have shipped a false claim that could have led to fake incidents in live feeds.
2. The real-guard walkthrough — five logical gaps that no consistency check would have flagged, because the document was internally consistent and still wrong.
3. The screenshots — proved one of my own requirements false and named three live consumers we had not listed.
4. The design and tone rejections — the version going out looks and reads nothing like the first one.
5. Insisting on the process itself — every defect found today was found because I demanded the review, not because I spotted it by reading.

**A fair summary:** AI did the production and the checking. I did the deciding, the judging, and the reality-testing. The value I added was mostly in refusing things — refusing unverified output, refusing an overclaim, refusing a heavy design, refusing a flow that did not survive contact with a real guard.
